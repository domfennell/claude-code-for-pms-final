Rook Dispatch · Release 4.2

A missed call was scored the same as a refusal — and nothing let it recover.

This is what actually happened, backed by the findings and the exact prompts that surfaced them, the fix we prototyped, and the skill that stops it shipping quietly again.

6 sessions 147 tickets read 1 root cause 4+ responders affected

What happened

Release 4.2 shipped two changes at once on 12 August — and one of them exposed a bug that had been sitting in the scoring logic, unnoticed, for years.

The release bundled two things. Ranking was changed to weight nearby responders higher, and the ping timeout was cut from 90 seconds to 60. Both looked like reasonable, separate tuning changes.

The timeout cut exposed a bug that was already there. In history.py, a missed ping — phone dead, no signal, too slow to answer — was scored exactly the same as a deliberate decline. And nothing let that score recover on its own.

Shorter timeout → more misses → more "refusals" → lower rank → fewer pings → no way back. A handful of responders got caught in this loop right after the release and never climbed out, while the company-wide number quietly looked fine again — because the same work just moved to people who hadn't been caught.

The company-wide number lied by averaging. Acceptance recovered from 54% back to 73% within weeks — but that's the system redistributing work to already-strong responders, not a fix. Four people stayed stuck at the floor the entire time.

The compounding bug: a short timeout feeds a scoring rule with no exit, so a few unlucky misses become permanent.

Why the company-wide number was misleading

Ping-level acceptance rate, company-wide — looks recovered. It isn't, for everyone.

That "recovery" is the same work being redistributed to already-strong responders — four people never came back up. Detail: 00-rook/feedback/4.2-writeup-for-eng-and-product.md.

How this doesn't happen again

A reusable skill, review-checklist, that checks any brief for the basics a bug like this slips through without.

Point it at any brief — /review-checklist <file> — and it checks four things, cold, on any document, not just this one:

✓Names who owns itSomeone is named to actually decide, not just listed as a stakeholder.
✓Says how we'll know it workedA real, checkable condition — not "make it better."
✓Keeps the same scope start to finishCatches scope creep slipped in mid-document.
✓Explains the problem before the fixSo a decision never gets asked for before the problem's understood.
Stress-tested against a deliberately bad, made-up brief with planted scope creep and an unnamed owner — caught both, cold, with no tuning toward this specific story. Then run against brief.md itself, which was missing a success condition and had its decisions ahead of the problem — both fixed as a direct result.
